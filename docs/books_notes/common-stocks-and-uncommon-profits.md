## 2. Core investment philosophy

Fisher's central thesis is that **the biggest gains come from holding a small number of truly outstanding companies for a very long time** — not from buying cheap stocks or from trading. He is explicit that the intelligent investor should not buy common stocks simply because they are cheap, but only if they give promise of *major* gain (p. 75). A company that qualifies on most of his fifteen points "could easily borrow money, at prevailing rates for its size company, up to the accepted top percentage of debt" — so for such companies the investor need not worry about dilution, and the question becomes whether growth will be financed without cancelling the shareholder's benefit (p. 75).

What he rejects: one-time profits from companies with a stationary or declining sales curve; "bargain hunting" on price alone; selling a good stock because it has risen; and the idea that diversification protects the uninformed. He is equally clear about what he cannot offer: his method is not for everyone. The scuttlebutt method and the fifteen points "work for growth stocks" and require time, access and judgement that most investors do not have (p. 34, 44).

The philosophy is built on **information advantage through legwork** (the "scuttlebutt" method) rather than on financial ratios. Ratios are a *check*, not a source: "the success of a stock purchase does not depend on what is generally known about a company at the time the purchase is made. Rather it depends upon what gets to be known about it after the stock has been bought" (p. 63).

## 3. Universe of applicability
- **Company types:** growth companies with "sizable increase in sales for at least several years" (p. 47); explicitly *not* cyclicals, *not* one-time-profit situations, *not* promotional companies. Small companies with "staggering possibilities of gain" are a separate, riskier class (p. 162).
- **Markets:** US-centric (the examples are Alcoa, Du Pont, Motorola, Texas Instruments, IBM, Dow, Ampex). The method is market-agnostic in principle but the scuttlebutt network is local.
- **Time horizon:** "ten or twenty-five years" (p. 48); growth should be judged in units of several years, not annually (p. 48).
- **Best conditions:** companies that are "fortunate because they are able" — able management that converts opportunity into sustained growth (p. 49).
- **Worst conditions:** promotional companies, companies with a static sales curve, and any situation where the investor cannot do the legwork. Fisher warns the average investor "just won't have much chance to apply" scuttlebutt (p. 45).

## 5. Decision rules (if-then)

- **IF** a company has products/services with multi-year sales potential **AND** management determined to keep developing new markets → **THEN** it is a candidate for the greatest gains. [EXPLICIT, p. 47–53]
- **IF** the investor cannot do the scuttlebutt legwork himself → **THEN** he should at least understand what is needed so he can select and evaluate a professional advisor who can. [EXPLICIT, p. 45]
- **IF** a mistake was made in the original purchase and the factual background is significantly less favorable than believed → **THEN** sell. This is reason 1 of only three. [EXPLICIT, p. 105]
- **IF** a fundamental change is taking place within the company → **THEN** sell. Reason 2. [EXPLICIT, p. 105]
- **IF** a better opportunity appears → **THEN** sell only after "the greatest care in trying to appraise accurately all elements of the situation", because the investor knows his current holding well and the new one not at all. [EXPLICIT, p. 106]
- **IF** the stock has had a huge advance and may be temporarily overpriced → **THEN** do not sell. "That which really matters is not to disturb a position that is going to be worth a great deal more later." [EXPLICIT, p. 111]
- **IF** a general market decline is feared → **THEN** do not sell an outstanding stock; the chance of being right is "still further diminished by the factor of the capital gains tax". [EXPLICIT, p. 109]
- **IF** a company is a small, high-potential/high-risk venture → **THEN** never put more than 5% of available funds into any one such company, and never invest funds you cannot afford to lose. [EXPLICIT, p. 162]
- **IF** a company fails on "many" of the fifteen points → **THEN** it does not meet his definition of a worthwhile investment. [EXPLICIT, p. 47]
- **IF** a company fails on "a very few" points → **THEN** it "could well be an investment bonanza". [EXPLICIT, p. 47]
- **IF** management clams up when troubles occur → **THEN** avoid (Point 14). [EXPLICIT, p. 76]
- **IF** management shows any self-dealing → **THEN** avoid regardless of other points. [EXPLICIT, p. 77–78]
- **IF** a company needs equity financing that will largely cancel existing shareholders' benefit → **THEN** avoid (Point 13). [EXPLICIT, p. 75]
- **IF** a company is promotional / has less than 2–3 years of commercial operation and one year of operating profit → **THEN** avoid. [EXPLICIT, p. 123]
- **IF** a stock is traded over-the-counter and the investor cannot get scuttlebutt → **THEN** be extra careful (Don't 2). [EXPLICIT, p. 125]
- **IF** the investor likes the "tone" of an annual report → **THEN** that is not a reason to buy (Don't 3). [EXPLICIT, p. 135]
- **IF** a stock's high price is due to a P/E that seems high → **THEN** do not assume it is overpriced; for a 20% grower, 35x earnings may be fine. [EXPLICIT, p. 111]
- **IF** the investor quibbles over eighths and quarters → **THEN** he is focusing on the wrong thing (Don't 5). [EXPLICIT, p. 137]

## 7. Position sizing and portfolio rules

- **Max position (small high-risk companies):** "larger investors should never at the time of the original investment put over 5 per cent of available funds into any one such company" [EXPLICIT, p. 162].
- **Min/max positions:** Fisher does not give a fixed number of holdings. He argues against both extremes: too few concentrates risk in a method that requires skill; too many dilutes the effect of the outstanding companies. "The amount, if any, of such securities in an investment list should vary according to the circumstances and goals of the particular investor" [EXPLICIT, p. 162].
- **Sector limits:** none explicit; the scuttlebutt method naturally spans industries.
- **Entry:** "When to Buy" — buy when the company qualifies on the points and the scuttlebutt confirms; do not postpone an attractive purchase out of fear of the general market [EXPLICIT, p. 89–97].
- **Exit:** only three reasons — (1) a mistake in the original purchase becomes clear, (2) a fundamental change in the company, (3) a genuinely better opportunity, and even then with great care [EXPLICIT, p. 105–106]. Otherwise hold: "once a stock has been properly selected and has borne the test of time, it is only occasionally that there is any reason for selling it at all" [EXPLICIT, p. 107].
- **Rebalancing:** none. Selling a good stock to switch is explicitly discouraged because of the tax and the information asymmetry [EXPLICIT, p. 106].
- **Cash policy:** not specified as a rule; the emphasis is on being fully invested in the right companies.
- **Dividends:** "The Hullabaloo about Dividends" — retained earnings must be examined on their own merit; a dividend is not inherently good or bad [EXPLICIT, p. 114–115].

## 8. Assumptions and prerequisites

- The investor (or his advisor) can **talk to competitors, vendors, customers, research scientists, trade-association executives and former employees** of a company, and can promise confidentiality [EXPLICIT, p. 46].
- The investor can **contact company officers** to fill gaps [EXPLICIT, p. 46].
- The investor has the **time and temperament** for multi-year holding and for the emotional self-control to admit mistakes [EXPLICIT, p. 105].
- The investor can **judge management quality** — the method is people-based, not ratio-based [EXPLICIT, p. 44].
- The market will eventually recognize sustained growth: "the influence on share prices of an upward trend of earnings multiplied by a comparable upward trend in the way each dollar of such earnings is valued" [EXPLICIT, p. 99].

## 9. Known limitations

- **Not for the average investor:** Fisher himself says most investors "just won't have much chance to apply" scuttlebutt [EXPLICIT, p. 45].
- **Requires access:** the method depends on informal networks that most investors lack [EXPLICIT, p. 44].
- **Requires patience:** the payoff is measured in decades [EXPLICIT, p. 48].
- **No valuation discipline:** Fisher explicitly refuses to say what is "overpriced" for a fast grower — "how can anyone say with even moderate precision just what is overpriced for an outstanding company with an unusually rapid growth rate?" [EXPLICIT, p. 111]. This is the biggest gap for a systematic implementation.
- **No sell discipline beyond three reasons:** the method is buy-and-hold almost by construction.
- **US-centric examples** from the 1950s–70s; the industries cited (aluminum, nylon, transistors, TV) are historical.
- **No quantitative thresholds:** none of the fifteen points has a numeric cutoff; all are qualitative judgements.

## 11. Distinguishing fingerprint

Fisher's unique contribution is the **scuttlebutt method**: the systematic collection of qualitative intelligence from a company's competitors, vendors, customers, former employees, and industry scientists, under a promise of confidentiality, to build a picture that no financial ratio can provide. Where Graham asks "is this cheap?" and Buffett asks "is this a moat?", Fisher asks **"what do the people who actually know this company say about it?"** — and he treats that answer as the primary input, with ratios as a secondary check. The fifteen points are the *structure* of that inquiry; scuttlebutt is the *engine*.

## 12. Methodology family

**Primary: QUALITY_COMPOUNDER**

Fisher is the archetypal quality-compounder: buy outstanding companies with able management, hold for decades, let the compounding work. The fifteen points are almost entirely about quality (management, R&D, sales, labor, integrity) with only one point (13) touching financing.

**Secondary: GARP** — the discussion of "fortunate and able" vs "fortunate because able" (p. 49) and the refusal to sell a 20% grower at 35x earnings (p. 111) show a growth-at-a-reasonable-price sensibility, but Fisher never frames it as a price discipline; he frames it as "don't disturb a position that is going to be worth a great deal more later."

## 13. Contradictions with common beliefs

| Common belief | This book's position | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| "Diversify to reduce risk" | Too much diversification dilutes the effect of the outstanding companies | The method requires concentration in a few names the investor knows deeply |
| "Sell when a stock has had a big run" | Do not sell an outstanding stock because it has risen | The next bull market should take it to a new peak; you will not know when to buy back |
| "Sell before a bear market" | Do not sell out of fear of a general decline | You are ignoring what you know (the company) to guess what you cannot know (the market) |
| "A high P/E means overpriced" | For a 20% grower, 35x earnings may be fine | "If the growth rate is so good that in another ten years the company might well have quadrupled, is it really of such great concern whether at the moment the stock might or might not be 35 per cent overpriced?" |
| "Dividends are the return" | Retained earnings must be judged on their own merit | Sometimes they add value, sometimes they are wasted, sometimes they are simply not the shareholder's |
| "Buy what is cheap" | Do not buy common stocks simply because they are cheap | Only buy if they promise *major* gain |
| "More information is always better" | Only information that changes the *future* matters | "it is not the profit margins of the past but those of the future that are basically important" |
