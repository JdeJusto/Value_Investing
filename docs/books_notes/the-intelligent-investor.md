## 2. Core investment philosophy

Graham's central thesis is that **investment is the purchase of a business at a price that provides a "margin of safety"** — and that most people who think they are investing are actually speculating. He opens by separating "investment" from "speculation" (p. 3) and insists the difference is not a matter of temperament or intent but of *method*: an investment operation is one which, upon thorough analysis, promises safety of principal and an adequate return (p. 2).

What makes a good investment is **not the price you pay relative to what the market thinks, but relative to what the business is worth**. The market is a voting machine in the short run and a weighing machine in the long run — the "Mr. Market" allegory (p. 26–27) is the mechanism that makes this operable: you are offered a daily price by a manic-depressive partner, and you are free to ignore him. The investor's edge is therefore *not* analytical brilliance but **temperament**: the willingness to buy when others are despondent and to refuse to buy when others are euphoric.

What he rejects: buying because a stock has risen ("the stock market report will tell you…"), paying generous prices for glamorous companies, following formulas that worked in the past, and the idea that the analyst can time the market. He is explicit that "postponing an attractive purchase because of fear of what the general market might do will, over the years, prove very costly" (p. 89–90).

## 3. Universe of applicability
- **Company types:** large, established companies for the *defensive* investor; smaller or out-of-favour companies for the *enterprising* (aggressive) investor; explicitly excludes companies the investor cannot analyse, and "promotional" ventures.
- **Markets:** US-centric (all examples are US industrials, rails, utilities). The criteria are market-agnostic in principle but the thresholds (100M sales) reflect the US economy of the 1960s–70s.
- **Time horizon:** long term; Graham expects the market to recognize value eventually and warns it can take longer than the investor's patience.
- **Best conditions:** depressed markets, when "a great proportion of ordinary shares are bargain issues" (p. 52); also individual bargains at any market level, provided the investor has the courage to act "in the teeth of a business depression" (p. 52).
- **Worst conditions:** euphoric markets, where his criteria "eliminarán a la inmensa mayoría de las acciones ordinarias como candidatas" (p. 113) and the investor must accept idleness.

## 5. Decision rules (if-then)

- **IF** industrial: sales ≥ $100M **AND** current ratio ≥ 2:1 **AND** LT debt ≤ NWC **AND** profitable in each of the last 10 years **AND** dividends uninterrupted for ≥ 20 years **AND** EPS grew ≥ 1/3 over the last decade **AND** P/E ≤ 15 **AND** P/BV ≤ 1,5 **THEN** it passes the defensive investor's seven criteria. [EXPLICIT, p. 112–114]
- **IF** P/E × P/BV > 22,5 → **THEN** reject, even if each multiple alone is acceptable. [EXPLICIT, p. 114]
- **IF** a stock's estimated intrinsic value is < 1,5× the market price → **THEN** it is not a "true bargain" for the enterprising investor. [EXPLICIT, p. 52]
- **IF** a stock trades below its net working capital → **THEN** it qualifies as a candidate bargain (second test). [EXPLICIT, p. 52–53]
- **IF** the general market is depressed → **THEN** buy; "la inteligencia de mostrar coraje en los mercados deprimidos queda reivindicada" [EXPLICIT, p. 52]
- **IF** the investor postpones an attractive purchase for fear of what the market might do → **THEN** "over the years, prove very costly". [EXPLICIT, p. 89]
- **IF** the investor cannot analyse a company → **THEN** stay out, whatever the price. [EXPLICIT, p. 112]
- **IF** a growth rate is extrapolated from the past → **THEN** distrust it; "la tasa de crecimiento durante el pasado, sorprendentemente, también" is not a predictor (p. 111, Zweig's commentary). [EXPLICIT, p. 111]

## 7. Position sizing and portfolio rules

- **Max position:** not fixed by formula, but the defensive investor's diversification is *implicit*: a diversified list of stocks that pass the seven criteria; "la propia lista diversificada, basada en los anteriores principios de selección, en combinación con cualesquiera otros criterios sensatos… debería obtener unos resultados suficientemente satisfactorios" (p. 113).
- **Min/max positions:** not specified numerically; the enterprising investor may concentrate more on bargains.
- **Sector limits:** none explicit.
- **Entry:** when price meets the criteria; do not wait for a market decline.
- **Exit:** the criteria are *screening* rules, not sell rules; Graham does not give a mechanical sell trigger. Zweig's commentary adds that the investor should re-examine holdings periodically.
- **Rebalancing:** not addressed mechanically.
- **Cash policy:** the defensive investor should not be forced to sell in a downturn; Graham recommends a 25–75% stock/bond ratio band adjusted to the market level ("el inversor debería mantener una división en partes iguales entre obligaciones y acciones" in normal times, p. 91) — the famous 50/50 baseline with bands.

## 8. Assumptions and prerequisites

- The investor can compute book value, working capital, P/E and P/BV from published statements — i.e. **financial statements are available and reasonably accurate**.
- The market is inefficient enough that bargains exist, but efficient enough that they are eventually recognized.
- The investor has the temperament to act against the crowd and to wait.
- For the enterprising investor: time and skill to estimate intrinsic value and to do the "great care" appraisal before switching holdings.
- The investor can distinguish investment from speculation — a distinction Graham considers "only understood by a small minority" (p. 107).

## 9. Known limitations

- **Dated thresholds:** $100M in sales is trivial today; the 20-year dividend record and the 2:1 current ratio screen out most modern growth companies.
- **No sell discipline:** the seven criteria are entry screens; the book gives no mechanical exit.
- **No portfolio construction beyond diversification:** no position sizing formula.
- **US-centric, mid-20th-century examples** (rails, utilities, industrials).
- **The 50/50 stock/bond rule is crude** and Zweig's commentary itself notes it is "not a formula" but a temperament aid.
- **No treatment of growth valuation:** Graham explicitly declines to value growth stocks; the enterprising investor is left with "a low multiplier plus something else".

## 11. Distinguishing fingerprint

Graham's unique contribution is the **margin of safety as a quantitative, multi-criterion screen**: a small set of hard numerical tests (size, liquidity, debt, dividend record, earnings growth, P/E, P/BV and their product) that can be applied mechanically to any company, plus the psychological device (Mr. Market) that tells the investor *why* he can act on those numbers when the crowd cannot. Where Fisher asks "what do people say about this company?" and Buffett asks "is there a moat?", Graham asks **"does the price leave enough room for error?"** — and he answers with a checklist, not a narrative.

## 12. Methodology family

**Primary: DEEP_VALUE**

Graham is the founder of deep value: buy below intrinsic value with a wide margin, demand financial strength and a long dividend record, and let the market converge. The net-working-capital bargain (price < current assets − total liabilities) is the purest expression.

**Secondary: GARP** — the combined P/E × P/BV test explicitly allows a higher multiple of one if the other is lower, which is a growth-at-a-reasonable-price logic. Zweig's commentary reinforces this: the criteria are designed to be "excluyentes en direcciones opuestas" (p. 113).

## 13. Contradictions with common beliefs

| Common belief | This book's position | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| "Growth stocks are the best investments" | Rejects paying generous prices for glamour companies | "la mayoría de ellos insistirán en que los inversores conservadores también deben estar dispuestos a pagar precios generosos por acciones de empresas selectas" — and he argues the opposite (p. 113) |
| "Time the market" | Do not postpone attractive purchases out of fear of the general market | "postponing an attractive purchase because of fear of what the general market might do will, over the years, prove very costly" (p. 89) |
| "High P/E means overpriced" | Only in combination with a high P/BV; the product must stay ≤ 22,5 | 9× earnings at 2,5× book passes; 15× at 1,5× is the ceiling (p. 114) |
| "Dividends are the return" | A 20-year unbroken record is a *screening* criterion, not a valuation input | Stability of the record signals financial strength and management policy (p. 113) |
| "Buy what is popular" | Buy what the market has ignored or abandoned | "El mercado es muy dado a hacer montañas con meros granos de arena" (p. 52) |
| "Diversification protects" | A diversified list of *qualifying* stocks protects; diversification alone does not | The criteria, not the count, provide the protection (p. 113) |
| "Sell when it is up" | Do not sell out of fear of a bear market or because the stock has risen | "How is the investor to know when to buy back?" (p. 109) |

## 14. Sample rules in pseudo-code

```
defensive_investor_screen:
if industrial and sales >= 100_000_000:
    return PASS
elif utility and assets >= 50_000_000:
    return PASS
else:
    return FAIL   # "too small for the defensive investor"

if current_assets / current_liabilities < 2.0:
    return FAIL   # liquidity test
if industrial and long_term_debt > net_working_capital:
    return FAIL
if utility and long_term_debt > 2 * equity:
    return FAIL
if any(deficit in last_10_years):
    return FAIL
if dividend_interrupted_in_last_20_years:
    return FAIL
if eps_growth_over_decade < 1/3:
    return FAIL   # using 3-year averages at each end
if price / avg_earnings_3y > 15:
    return FAIL
if price / book_value > 1.5:
    return FAIL
if (price / avg_earnings_3y) * (price / book_value) > 22.5:
    return FAIL   # the combined test governs even if each alone passes
return PASS

bargain_test:  # enterprising investor
if estimated_intrinsic_value / market_price >= 1.5:
    return PASS   # "at least 50% above the price"
if market_price < current_assets - total_liabilities:
    return PASS   # net-working-capital bargain
return FAIL

market_allegory:  # Mr. Market
if mr_market_quote < intrinsic_value * safety_factor:
    return BUY
elif mr_market_quote > intrinsic_value * (1 + safety_factor):
    return IGNORE   # not necessarily sell; you own the business, not the quote
else:
    return HOLD
```
